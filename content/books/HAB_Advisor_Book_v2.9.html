How to Use This Book

It's not a binder for the drawer. It's a training weapon for the bench.

This book is your operating manual from your first day as a Rookie to the day customers ask for you by name and you train other advisors. Every script inside has been battle-tested across thousands of real calls. The words are exact. The order matters. The psychology is deliberate. Your job is not to read it — your job is to absorb it until the words disappear and the instinct remains.

One distinction before anything else: the sales PROCESS and the sales OBJECTIVES are two different things. The process is what leads: get the car into the shop, secure the appointment. The objectives — establish rapport, establish credibility, educate the customer — are pursued through and after the close, never in front of it. Every script in this book is built in that order, starting with the very first exchange of the inbound call.

The Core Principle

Sales is not persuasion. Sales is the systematic reduction of uncertainty.
We do not sell repairs. We manufacture certainty — and certainty buys repairs by itself.

Every customer who calls a shop is operating in a fog. Uncertain whether the noise is dangerous. Uncertain whether they're about to be taken advantage of. Uncertain whether the price will be fair, whether the shop can be trusted, about their car, about themselves. That fog is the real problem we solve. Repairs are simply the byproduct.

Your job is to be the lighthouse. Each of the eight steps in this system removes one specific kind of uncertainty. When all eight are removed, the customer doesn't feel sold — they feel guided. And guided customers come back, refer their friends, and never haggle.

Customer EntersCustomer Leaves
Uncertain · Fearful · Defensive · ConfusedEducated · Confident · Safe · Loyal

Sales Process vs. Sales Objectives

Two different things, never to be confused. The sales process is the sequence of actions that gets the car into the shop and the work authorized — it leads, always. The sales objectives are the relationship outcomes — rapport, credibility, education — pursued through and after every close, never in front of one. The process secures the appointment; the objectives make the customer glad it did. When an advisor recites a shop introduction before answering the customer's question, the objectives have been allowed to block the process. That is backwards, and this book corrects it starting with the very first line of the inbound call.

The Six Sales Objectives

Underneath the 8 process steps live 6 unchanging objectives. Securing the vehicle leads the list — every other objective is pursued through and after it. Every move you make — every word, pause, walk, and inspection — must serve at least one. If a behavior serves none of them, it's waste motion. Cut it.

Status — The Servant Is Not the Subordinate

Everything in this book is service. We hold the door, we fetch the coffee, we shuttle the customer home, and we photograph every finding so the customer never has to take our word for anything. The advisor is the servant of the customer — and the advisor is never the customer's subordinate. Those are two different things, and the entire system depends on keeping them separate.

Think about the professionals whose advice you actually take. They serve you completely — and they speak to you as equals, plainly and directly. The moment a professional starts talking UP to the customer, their advice stops sounding like expertise and starts sounding like a request. An advisor who lowers their own status to elevate the customer's has not made the customer feel important. They have made the customer feel unadvised.

We never lower our status to elevate the customer's. We serve as equals — that is what makes the service worth anything.

The "Just" Leak — How Status Escapes One Word at a Time

Status doesn't collapse in one sentence. It leaks — and the most common leak in this industry is the word "just." "I'm just calling to follow up." "I just wanted to check in." "Sorry to bother you, I just had a quick question." Underneath, every one of those sentences says the same two things: my time is not as valuable as yours, and is it okay that I'm talking to you? The customer hears an apology for the call before they have heard the reason for the call. We never do this.

The anti-pattern has a name: Status Shrinking — diminishing language that shrinks the advisor so the customer feels bigger. It reads as politeness. It performs as surrender. It gets coached out of every advisor in this system, because in a few hours this same voice will ask the customer to trust it with their family's brakes.

Status Shrinking (never)Equal Status (always)
"I'm just calling to follow up.""I'm calling with an update on your vehicle."
"I just wanted to check in on the estimate.""I told you I'd call when the estimate was ready. It's ready, and I'll walk you through it."
"Sorry to bother you, I just had a quick question.""I have one question for you before my technician moves forward."
"Whenever you get a chance, no rush.""Call me back by 2 o'clock so we can keep your repair moving."
"I'm just the service advisor.""I'm your service advisor, and I'm handling your vehicle personally."
🟣 Psychology Decoder · Why status leaks kill authorization Every presentation in this system ends with the customer accepting professional judgment they can see evidence for but cannot fully evaluate themselves. That transfer of trust only happens between equals — nobody hands their family's safety to a voice that asked permission to speak. When the advisor opens with "I'm just calling to," the education in Step 6 has already been re-labeled: it will land as a pitch from a salesperson instead of a finding from a professional. The words that follow can be perfect and it won't matter. Status is set in the first sentence, and every sentence after it is heard through that setting.

Why Rapport and Credibility Exist — The Right to Shoot Straight

Here is the deeper why behind Modules 1 and 2 — the reason both of them exist at all: customers like directness most of all. More than warmth, more than politeness, more than a deal, what a customer wants from a shop is a straight answer from someone who knows. But directness cannot be deployed cold. A direct sentence from a stranger is an attack; the same sentence from a trusted advisor is a gift. You cannot be direct with someone until rapport and credibility are established.

That is what the first two modules are actually FOR. Rapport is not friendliness for its own sake, and credibility is not bragging rights. They are the license to shoot straight. Ninety seconds of rapport and a ninety-second tour buy you the right to tell a customer their brake pads are done and that the car should not carry their kids until the pads are replaced — and to have that received as care instead of pressure.

Rapport and credibility are not the product. They are the license. The product is the straight answer the customer came for.

Tone — The Delivery System

Status is the posture; tone is the delivery. The same words at a different pace and pitch are a different message. "You've got to have it inspected" delivered fast and flat is a line being read. Delivered slower, a note lower, with genuine concern, it is a professional worrying about your car. The customer does not simply hear what you say — the customer hears what your tone tells them you mean. The skill is matching the delivery to what the customer needs to hear in that moment.

The MomentThe Delivery
Concern discovery ("What kind of concern are you having?")Slow down. Drop the pitch. Soft and curious — an invitation, not an interrogation.
The urgency line ("Oh my goodness...")Energy up, genuine concern. The words carry the urgency; the tone is what makes it real.
Investment + coverageEven, steady, unhurried. A calm statement of fact from someone with nothing to hide.
Objections and Boss BattlesSlower and softer, curious rather than adamant. A calm question outperforms a firm rebuttal.
Recovery (L.A.S.T.)The lowest, slowest tone you own. Match the customer's gravity, never their volume.
⚪ Pro Tip · The recommended study: Jeremy Miner For tone, the HAB-recommended outside study is Jeremy Miner's NEPQ material — The New Model of Selling and the NEPQ Black Book. Steal four things from it: slowing the pace, lowering the tone, softer curious approaches instead of firm adamant statements, and matching your delivery to the moment so the customer HEARS what you need them to hear. Ten minutes of study a week, one tone behavior drilled per morning huddle.

Mission Brief

Rapport is not small talk. Rapport is the moment a stranger's nervous system relaxes enough to receive information. Every other step in this system — credibility, custody, inspection, presentation, close — is gated by this one. If the customer is still in fight-or-flight when you start talking about a water pump, they will not hear the water pump. They will hear a salesperson.

So the first 90 seconds are not a warm-up. They are the highest-leverage 90 seconds of the entire sale. Spend them deliberately.

A customer in rapport authorizes work. A customer out of rapport authorizes nothing — not even a free inspection.
🟣 Psychology Decoder · The deeper why — rapport buys the right to shoot straight Remember what rapport is FOR (Chapter 0.5): customers like directness most of all, but directness cannot be deployed cold. A direct sentence from a stranger is an attack; the same sentence from a trusted advisor is a gift. These 90 seconds are not the product — they are the license that lets every straight answer later in the call land as care instead of pressure. And while you build it, hold your status: warm, serving, and equal. Never shrinking.

The Psychology — Why Rapport Works

When a stranger calls or walks in, their brain runs a sub-second threat assessment. Two questions get answered before any conscious thought: Is this person safe? and Are they like me? If the answer to either is "no," the reasoning brain goes offline and every word out of your mouth gets filtered through suspicion. Rapport flips both answers to "yes" in under 90 seconds — not by tricking the customer, but by deliberately producing the signals their nervous system is scanning for. Three mechanisms do the heavy lifting:

MechanismHow It Works
Mirror NeuronsHumans unconsciously copy the emotional state of the person in front of them. Smile, and they smile. Tense up at the price, and they tense up. You set the emotional thermostat for the room.
Limbic ResonancePace and rhythm sync between two people in conversation. A calm, confident advisor pulls a panicked customer down into calm. A rushed advisor speeds the customer up into hesitation. Pace controls trust.
ReciprocityGive first — a warm greeting, a glass of water, eye contact — and the brain creates a tiny obligation. That obligation is what later gets cashed in as "sure, let's do the inspection."
🟣 Psychology Decoder · Why "Absolutely, you've called the right place" lands harder than "Hi, how can I help?" The brain is scanning for relief. "Absolutely" answers the customer's exact question — their stated concern is acknowledged in the very first word they hear back. "You've called the right place" is a one-sentence relief delivery: it tells the nervous system "you can stop searching, you can stop comparing, the threat is resolved." The body literally exhales. From that exhale forward, everything you say is heard by the reasoning brain instead of being filtered by suspicion — which is exactly why the closing ask ("Is now a good time to bring your vehicle in?") rides in the same breath. This is why we never replace this line with "Sure, we can do that." "Sure" is information. "Right place" is relief. And relief is the moment to close.

🟣 Psychology Decoder · Why "AND I" — the personal accountability frame The line is not "my technician will inspect it." It is "my certified technician AND I." Those two words are a personal promise, and you keep every part of it: (1) you make sure the technician understands the customer's concern exactly as the customer stated it; (2) you inspect the vehicle WITH the technician and document what you both see; (3) YOU personally build the written estimate — the tech doesn't; and (4) you use the line to educate the customer on that workflow, so they know exactly what happens to their car and are never just handed off and left waiting two hours in the lobby wondering. The customer hears one human being take ownership of their vehicle and their concern. That is the first step of rapport — accountability before acquaintance. And once the promise is made, circle back to the close: "Is now a good time to bring it in?"

🟣 Psychology Decoder · Why East-West beats "What time works?" Open-ended scheduling forces the customer to pick from infinity — and the brain's default response to that work is hesitation. Two specific options collapse infinity into a binary, and most humans answer a binary on the first try. The rules: always two options at specific off-hour times, one earlier and one later ("I've got 11:15 or 2:15 open today"); state them firmly, no "uh" or "maybe"; close with a choice question; and when the concern is already on the table, let the urgency lead into the options ("With that squeak coming from your brake system, we don't want to leave it unchecked long"). If they hesitate: "What's on your mind?" If they ask for a different time, re-anchor: "The closest options I have are 2:15 or 2:45." Bonus: East-West builds a balanced appointment book and quietly signals your calendar is real and full — an authority cue.

Now the Teaching — Why the Quick Close Works

🟣 Psychology Decoder · Answer first, close second, nothing before either A customer who opens with "Do you work on brakes?" is not asking for a shop history — they are asking for permission. The visit is already in their head; your job is to say yes to it before uncertainty regrows. "Absolutely" answers their exact question — their stated concern is acknowledged in the very first word they hear back. "You've called the right place" delivers the relief. "Is now a good time to bring your vehicle in?" closes. One breath, three jobs. Every sentence you deliver before that close gives doubt time to re-form — and can turn a hot caller into a comparison shopper. The quick close skips nothing that matters: rapport and credibility still happen, after the yes — in the introduction for first-timers, in the shop news for returning customers, at the counter and on the tour for everyone.
🟣 Psychology Decoder · The concern question, the urgency line, and the "AND I" promise On a vague or quote call, three moves run in a fixed chain. First, the concern question: "What kind of concern are you having with your brakes?" — the quote is not the reason they called; the squeak is. Second, the urgency line: "Oh my goodness, when you have a squeak coming from your brake system, you've got to bring it in and have it inspected by one of our ASE-certified technicians" — the concern they just handed you becomes the urgency, and the urgency becomes the close. Third, if they push on price: "At no charge to you, my certified technician AND I will inspect your complete brake system and provide you a written estimate" — a gift instead of a number, sealed with a personal promise. Then you circle back to the exact same close, in the exact same words. The repetition is deliberate: it signals calm certainty, and it keeps the call pointed at the only outcome that matters — the vehicle in the shop. Never a number.
🟣 Psychology Decoder · Why "AND I" is the first step of rapport "My certified technician AND I" is a claim of personal accountability, and you back it with four behaviors: you make sure the technician understands the customer's concern exactly as stated; you inspect the vehicle WITH the technician and document the findings together; YOU build the written estimate personally — the tech never does; and you walk the customer through that workflow so they know what happens next and are never just handed off and left waiting two hours in the lobby. The customer's nervous system hears one accountable human, not a process. That is rapport doing its job inside the close. After the line, always circle back: "Is now a good time to bring it in?"
🔴 Stop · What the quick close is NOT It is not permission to quote — the answer to a price question is still never a number over the phone; the concern question and the no-charge "and I" line are the only answers a price question ever gets. Never educate before there is evidence. The customer's perception when they hang up is simple: "yes, I'm getting my oil change done." The Psychology — Why Credibility Works

The Psychology of Custody

The single most under-valued asset in the shop is the car in our bay. We cannot inspect a car we don't have. We cannot sell repairs on a car we don't have. We cannot build a relationship through a phone — only across a counter and a coffee. Custody is the gateway to every other objective in this system. The corollary: the second the vehicle is in the bay, we have also done a quieter but equally important thing — we have separated the customer from the vehicle. The customer is in the waiting room or in an Uber. They are no longer staring at the dashboard. Their emotional attachment to the symptom decays. Logical decisions become possible. Separation is what enables education. And once we have custody, the price conversation is fundamentally different — the customer has invested time and trust.

Our job is to convert symptom language into concern language, gently, without making the customer feel stupid.

The Root Concern Rule — Never Write a Job on a Self-Diagnosis

Concern vs. symptom has a harder cousin: the customer who names the service and says yes to coming in. The booking is secured, the call feels done — and this is exactly where jobs get written on someone else's diagnosis. The Root Concern Rule: even when the customer names a service and agrees to come in, you still find the TRUE root concern before writing the job. Close first, then ask why. Two reasons, both expensive: we don't inherit somebody else's problems, and we don't fix a self-diagnosis — a repair that doesn't fix the customer's actual problem doesn't just waste their money, it puts the shop on the hook for a failure it never diagnosed.

And one guardrail on top (Ch 1.5 — Say Yes, Get Them In — No Pre-Education): that entire paragraph is for YOU, not for the customer. On the phone, the Root Concern Rule runs on questions, urgency, and value framing only. You never tell the customer their oil change may not fix it, their engine may be the wrong call, or their diagnosis may be mistaken. They said what they want; you say yes, you get them in, and the inspection produces the evidence that earns the education.

Why Inspection Exists — It's Not About the Repair

The inspection is not for the technician. The technician already knows what is wrong with the car. The inspection — specifically the digital inspection with photos, video, and notes — is for the customer. It is a piece of evidence we hand them in Step 6 so they can see what we see. Selling the Inspection Too Early

The most common bad habit in advising is pitching the full digital vehicle inspection too soon. It feels like transparency. It reads as an upsell. Customers call for a specific service precisely BECAUSE they fear being sold more than they asked for — lead with the inspection and you confirm that fear before the car is even in the bay.

The classic version is the tire-shop story. A customer books tires. Walking past the bay, they see their own car with the hood up: "Why is my hood open? I brought it in for tires." Nothing dishonest happened — the shop inspects every car — but the sequence failed: nobody wrapped the inspection inside the customer's own plan, so the open hood reads as the upsell starting. A sequence failure creates the exact resistance the DVI exists to remove.

The single most common reason a customer says "let me think about it" is not price — it's overwhelm. The Two Frameworks Inside Every Base

Framework A · Good · Good · Bad

Two positives prime the brain into a receptive state. The third statement (the cost or concern) then lands inside an open mind instead of a defensive one. Lead with bad news and you trigger fight-or-flight before the customer hears a single solution.

Example: "Good news: I have great DVI documentation. Good news: we identified the cause. The finding: the water pump is leaking."

Framework B · P · R · S

Problem · Risk · Solution. Every finding gets stated in this exact order, no skipping. The Risk step is the one most advisors omit — and it's the one that converts information into urgency. Without Risk, the customer doesn't know why to act now.

Follow PRS with the Total Investment, then the Coverage (warranty), then a tee-up sentence.

🔴 Stop · Do NOT mix bases on the same call Kitchen-sinking is the practice of dumping every finding — safety + maintenance + recovery + upsell — into one call. It feels efficient. It destroys trust. The customer hears a list of expenses and concludes "they're trying to get me." Each base is its own micro-conversation, with its own authorization, hung up between bases. Re-call for the next base. Slower per call — and a meaningfully higher close rate.

The Five Universal Rules of Objection Handling

  1. Never argue. Arguing tells the customer they were wrong to feel the way they feel. Even if you win the argument, you've lost the relationship.
  2. Acknowledge first, always. "I completely understand" / "I hear you" / "Fair enough." This is not concession — it's permission to be heard.
  3. Find the real concern. Clarifying questions: "Is it more than you expected, or are you comparing it to another estimate?" Information beats persuasion.
  4. Pivot to value or risk, never to discount. The instant you discount in response to an objection, you have trained the customer to objection-shop you forever.
  5. Re-ask for the close. Every objection-handled exchange must end with another move toward authorization. Otherwise you've just had a conversation.

Mirror the emotion, name the underlying concern, then bridge to the script. Never use "but."

🔴 Stop · The Reinforcement Rule — the Short Stop never contradicts the advisor The Short Stop must NEVER contradict what the advisor already told the customer. Not on the finding, not on the risk, not on the recommendation. The moment the second voice disagrees with the first, the customer hears one thing: this shop is making things up — and the advisor's trustworthiness, the asset every future visit depends on, is destroyed on the spot. The Short Stop REINFORCES first: the presented repair is the proper solution, exactly as presented. A second voice confirming the same story is what proves nobody is making anything up. Only after the reinforcement lands does the collaborative problem-solving open — showing the customer we're willing to work with them, not a hard take-it-or-leave-it wall. Reinforce first. Problem-solve second. No exceptions.
🟣 Psychology Decoder · The Honest Disarm "Second opinion" is not a price objection — it is a trust objection wearing a price costume. The customer is braced for you to defend the number, because a shop that defends its number sounds like a shop hiding its margin. So you do the last thing they expect: you agree. "We are able to do this cheaper" is true, disarming, and instantly re-classifies you as the honest party in the conversation — which is exactly what the customer was about to drive across town looking for. The honesty comes packaged with the tradeoff, stated plainly: cheaper parts reduce the price AND may reduce the warranty. Never sell the cheaper tier silently; the customer chooses it with eyes open or not at all.

Mission Brief

Every shop will have failures. A part won't arrive on time. A noise will come back. A bill will land higher than the estimate. A tech will leave a smudge on the seat. Failure is a constant. What is variable is the recovery. The L.A.S.T. method — Listen, Acknowledge, Solve, Thank — is the four-beat combo that turns a hot counter into a retained customer, at the exact moment the customer is most ready to either leave or deepen the relationship.

A customer who complains and is heard becomes more loyal than one who never had a problem at all. The complaint is the opening. The recovery is the bond.

The Psychology — Why Recovery Outperforms Smooth Service

The human brain has a negativity bias: bad experiences imprint roughly 2–4× stronger than good ones. That sounds like terrible news for a shop with a complaint — but it's the unlock. Because the imprint is so strong, when you reverse the experience with a great recovery, the recovery itself imprints with the same intensity. The customer no longer remembers the failure as a standalone event — they remember the recovery as the defining moment. This is the documented Service Recovery Paradox: customers whose problems are well-handled report higher loyalty and referral rates than customers who never had a problem at all.

MechanismHow It Works
Validation NeedAn upset customer's primary need is not the fix — it's the feeling of being heard. Jump to the fix and you skip the only step that actually resolves the emotion.
Effort AsymmetryThe customer expects the bare minimum. When you do meaningfully more — or even visibly try — the gap registers as care, and care is what loyalty is made of.
Story ConversionEvery customer will tell a story about this interaction. L.A.S.T. gives them a hero arc ("there was a problem, but they fixed it like champs") instead of a tragedy ("don't go there"). The story is your future business.
🟣 Psychology Decoder · Why this isn't an excuse to cause problems The Recovery Paradox is not permission to fail on purpose. The lift is real, but it only applies to well-handled recoveries — and only sometimes outperforms baseline. The takeaway is not "create complaints." It's "treat every complaint as the highest-leverage moment of the relationship" — because that is exactly what it is.

The Four Beats — In Order, Every Time

L.A.S.T. is a sequence, not a menu. Reorder it and it stops working. Skip a beat and the customer feels unheard. The most common failure is racing to S (Solve) without doing L and A — that's the move that makes customers escalate to Yelp.

🔴 Stop · The banned word is "policy" Never use the word "policy" during a recovery. It tells the customer "you don't matter, the rule does." Drills — Build the Reflex

🟢 Drill 9.A · Silence Tolerance (60-second reps) Setup: partner plays an upset customer venting for 60 full seconds. Your only allowed words: "okay," "I understand," "go ahead." No interrupting.
Score: did you make the full 60 seconds without defending, explaining, or jumping to solve?
Goal: 5 consecutive clean reps. The single hardest recovery skill to acquire.
🟢 Drill 9.B · The BUT Buster Setup: trainer reads a complaint. You acknowledge in 1–2 sentences without using "but," "however," or "although."
Score: clean acknowledgment? Emotion validated before any explanation?
Goal: 8/10 reps clean, then promote to the live floor.
🟢 Drill 9.C · Specificity Drill Setup: trainer states a complaint. You respond with a solve that has three components: who owns it, what exact next step, by when.
Score: all three present? Time-bound? Owned by you, not "someone will"?
Goal: 10 consecutive reps with all three.
🟢 Drill 9.D · The Full Combo (hot-counter roleplay) Setup: full live scenario, partner or AI in hot-customer mode — escalating, emotional, difficult. Run the complete L → A → S → T sequence.
Score (1–5 each beat): Listen quality + Acknowledge clarity + Solve specificity + Thank delivery. 16+/20 = mastery.
Goal: 3 consecutive scenarios scoring 16+. This is what coaches a Level-4 advisor into Level 5.
🟡 Mastery Check · Level 9 — Recovery Mastery (Gate to Champion)
  • Listened for at least 30 seconds without interrupting, defending, or explaining.
  • Acknowledged the customer's emotion before stating any facts.
  • Did not use the words "but," "however," or "policy."
  • Offered a specific, time-bound, owned next step.
  • Did not discount or comp before solving the actual problem.
  • Ended with a sincere Thank that reframed the complaint as a gift.
  • Customer left committed to a next step (return visit, callback, or resolution).
Pass all 7 across three live recoveries with documented retention → Level 5 (Champion) certified.

1 · Brakes — Front Pads & Rotors (Safety)

Scenario: customer came in for a brake squeal. DVI confirms front pads at 2mm with rotor scoring.

🟨 Base-1 ISO · Front Brakes

1. "Hi [Customer], this is [Advisor] from [Shop]. Thanks for your patience, is now a good time to go over your vehicle?"

2. "I personally walked the vehicle with our certified tech so I could give you a clear picture of what's going on with the braking system."

3. "Were you able to view the photos and the brake-measurement video on the digital inspection report I sent?"

4. "You brought the vehicle in because of the squealing when braking. We've fully inspected the system."

5. "The good news is the rear brakes still have plenty of life left, and the brake fluid tested clean."

6. "More good news, we caught the front brakes right at the safety threshold and not past it, which means we can replace the components without damage to the caliper or hydraulic system."

7. "If left as-is, the front pads are at 2 millimeters, that means metal-on-metal is the next stop, which destroys the rotors and can compromise stopping distance. The proper repair is to replace the front pads and rotors, perform a full brake system service including hardware and fluid bleed, and road-test under load to confirm."

8. "The total investment for today's work, parts and labor included, is $[XXX]."

9. "This work is fully covered by our 3-year / 36,000-mile nationwide warranty on both parts and labor."

10. "While your vehicle is here, we'll finish the full bumper-to-bumper inspection and I'll reach out if anything else needs attention. Is this still the best number to reach you?"

🟣 Psychology Decoder · Why "metal-on-metal" lands harder than "worn out" "Worn out" is abstract. "Metal-on-metal" is a vivid mental image the customer can hear in their head. Concrete sensory language activates the same brain regions as the actual event — which is why specific physical descriptions move customers to authorize faster than generic ones.

2 · Suspension — CV Axle Boot Leak (2nd-Base Discovery)

Scenario: customer came in for an oil change. DVI finds the CV axle boot torn with grease slung on inner suspension.

Inside of a few thousand miles that will destroy the CV joint, which can fail under acceleration and leave you stranded. The proper repair is to replace the CV axle assembly, inspect the inner CV joint and surrounding components, and verify alignment."

8. "The total updated investment, including the oil change you already authorized, is $[XXX]."

9. "Everything is covered by our 3-year / 36,000-mile nationwide warranty, parts and labor."

10. "With the axle work in progress I'll have the tech finish the remaining inspection items and loop back if anything else surfaces. Still the best number?"

🟣 Psychology Decoder · Why 3rd base ends with a real opt-out Beat 10 on a 3rd-base call explicitly offers to defer. This isn't weakness — it's positioning. Giving the customer the deferral option increases the same-day authorization rate because it removes the salesperson energy.

Group I · Anti-Patterns (get these coached OUT)

Anti-PatternWhy It's Bad
Quoting prices on the phoneLoses both the sale and the relationship. Crashes appointment rate. Earn the appointment first.
Scripting past the stated concernThe customer asks "do you work on brakes?" and the advisor recites the shop introduction instead of answering. Answer the question, close for the visit, introduce after the yes.
Closing a quote call blindThe request was vague or a quote and the advisor closed (or countered on price) without asking the concern question. No concern, no urgency; no urgency, weak close.
Writing the job on a self-diagnosisThe customer named a service, the advisor wrote it without asking why. Inherits someone else's problem, and the shop owns the failure when the "fix" doesn't fix it. Run the Root Concern Rule.
Saying "not just X" — anywhere"We'll inspect your complete oil system, not just change the oil" subliminally tells the customer NO to the thing they asked for and insinuates EXTRA — and extra means extra cost in the customer's mind, an undisclosed upsell that creates distrust. The construction is banned in ALL customer-facing dialogue, in every script and every boss battle, not only on the phone. State the value affirmatively; the no-charge line needs no justification.
Status Shrinking (the "just" leak)"I'm just calling to follow up" lowers the advisor's status to elevate the customer's — it says "my time is not as valuable as yours; is it okay that I'm talking to you?" The advisor is the servant, never the subordinate. Kill the word "just" and state the reason for the call as an equal: "I'm calling with an update on your vehicle."
Justifying the free inspectionA why-tail on the no-charge line ("so we know exactly what that light is telling us") re-opens the fear the line just closed. Deliver it clean, then close for the visit.
Selling the inspection too earlyBooking the appointment with the DVI, or pitching it before the customer's concern is handled. Book with the concern; present the DVI after the first approval, inside a waiting window, or via the shop introduction video. Coaches audit WHEN, not just whether.
Offering the waiting area at intakeHands the customer a comfortable way to stay attached to the vehicle — the separation objective fails. Run the keys moment; if they still wait, call the audible.
Pre-educating on the phoneThe advisor warns or hints, before any inspection, that the customer's requested service may not fix the problem. It voices the customer's fear, kills the yes, and guesses without evidence. Say yes, get them in; educate after the inspection produces evidence.
Generic "how can I help you" energyThe customer needs relief, not a menu. Use The Relief Line.
Skipping the shop tourPrice objections triple. Trust craters.
Kitchen-SinkingDumping all findings into one call. Halves authorization rate. Run the bases.
Skipping Risk in the PRSPhoto + Solution alone is just a quote; "let me think about it" becomes the standard reply.
Pausing after InvestmentTriggers sticker shock. Must flow into Coverage immediately.
Arguing with objectionsWins the argument, loses the customer.
Softening Red to YellowThe spec question is binary. Have the courage to call it what it is.
Defending before ListeningRecovery's #1 killer. The emotion can't drain through your explanation.
"I understand, BUT…"The word "but" deletes everything before it.
Using the word "policy"Tells the customer the rule matters more than they do. Banned in recovery.
Discounting as the first moveTrains customers that complaining = discounts.
Vague recoveries"We'll get back to you" instead of "I'll call you by 3pm."
